Quartz Photonic Hybrid Energy System (QPHES)



The QPHES is a rooftop hybrid energy system that combines electricity generation with thermal energy recovery. 
Rather than allowing solar panels to become hot and waste heat, the system transfers that heat into a closed-loop water circuit, 
storing it inside a large insulated thermal tank for space heating and domestic hot water.



1. Rooftop QPHES Array



For a 2,000 sq ft house:



Roof collector area: 320–360 sq ft

Number of QPHES panels: 10 panels

Individual panel dimensions:

4 ft × 8 ft

32 sq ft each

Total active collector area:

320 sq ft



Each panel performs three functions simultaneously:



Generate electricity

Capture waste heat

Protect the photovoltaic cells from weather

2. Internal Structure of Each QPHES Panel

Layer 1 — Optical Quartz Lens



Material:



High-purity fused silica (SiO₂)



Purpose:



Transmits visible light to the photovoltaic cells.

Passes infrared energy into the thermal collection layers.

Resists UV degradation, hail, snow, and thermal expansion.



Quartz is chosen because it remains optically clear over decades and tolerates much higher temperatures than conventional glass.



Layer 2 — Anti-Reflective Coating



Material:



Silicon dioxide

Titanium dioxide nanocoating



Purpose:



Reduces reflection.

Allows more sunlight to enter.

Self-cleans with rain.

Layer 3 — Spectral Management Layer



Purpose:



Separates sunlight into two energy paths:



Visible wavelengths:



→ Sent to the photovoltaic cells.



Infrared wavelengths:



→ Directed toward the thermal absorber.



This allows both electrical and thermal energy to be harvested from the same sunlight.



Layer 4 — High-Efficiency Photovoltaic Cells



Material:



Monocrystalline silicon

Copper busbars

Silver contacts



Purpose:



Generate electricity.



During operation these cells naturally warm up.



Instead of losing this heat to the outside air, QPHES captures it.



Layer 5 — Copper Heat Spreader



Material:



Oxygen-free copper



Purpose:



Immediately absorbs heat from the back of every solar cell.



Copper rapidly equalizes temperature across the panel, preventing localized overheating.



Layer 6 — Thermal Transfer Plate



Material:



Aluminum alloy

Graphite thermal interface sheet



Purpose:



Moves heat uniformly toward the cooling channels.



Layer 7 — Thermal Pipe Network



This is the heart of the heating system.



Inside every panel is a network of flattened copper tubing.



Typical tubing:



Copper

½ inch outside diameter

Approximately 50–70 feet of tubing per panel, arranged in a serpentine pattern.



As water circulates through these tubes, it absorbs heat from the copper spreader and thermal plate.



The water never contacts the photovoltaic cells directly; it flows through sealed tubing embedded beneath them.



Layer 8 — Insulation Layer



Material:



Aerogel blanket

High-density mineral wool



Purpose:



Prevents heat from escaping upward into the atmosphere.



Nearly all recovered heat is directed into the circulating water.



Layer 9 — Structural Frame



Material:



Extruded aluminum

Stainless steel fasteners



Purpose:



Supports the panel while resisting corrosion and weather.



3. Thermal Water Circuit



The heated water leaves the roof through insulated supply pipes.



Typical pipe:



1-inch insulated PEX or copper

Closed-loop circulation

Glycol-water mixture in freezing climates



The water flows continuously through the roof collectors whenever useful heat is available.



4. Thermal Storage Tank



For a 2,000 sq ft house, a recommended storage volume is:



800–1,000 gallons



A practical design target is:



1,000 gallons



This size provides a useful amount of stored heat for overnight operation in many winter conditions.



Construction:



Stainless steel inner tank

Polyurethane foam insulation (4–6 inches)

Aluminum outer jacket



Typical dimensions:



Diameter: ~5–6 ft

Height: ~8–9 ft



Location:



Basement utility room (preferred)

Mechanical room

Attached garage

Underground insulated vault (optional)

5. Heat Exchanger



Inside the storage tank is a heat exchanger.



Material:



Copper

Stainless steel



Purpose:



Separates the rooftop collector loop from the home's heating loop if desired, allowing different fluids (e.g., glycol on the roof and water indoors).



6. Home Heating Distribution



The roof pipes do not continue directly through the walls to heat the rooms.



Instead, the hot water in the storage tank feeds one or more heating systems.



Option A — Radiant Floor Heating (recommended)



Hot water circulates through PEX tubing installed beneath the floor.



Typical tubing:



½-inch PEX

Loops of 250–300 ft

Multiple zones



Warmth rises evenly through the rooms, providing quiet, uniform heating.



Option B — Hydronic Baseboard Radiators



Hot water flows through compact radiators installed along exterior walls, warming the air by natural convection.



Option C — Air Handler



Hot water passes through a finned heat exchanger inside the home's ductwork.



A blower moves air across the heat exchanger, distributing warm air through existing ceiling or floor vents.



In this configuration, the heat is carried by water to the air handler, not by roof pipes inside the walls.



7. Additional Heat Sources Within the Panel



Beyond the thermal pipe network, the panel also recovers heat from:



Photovoltaic cell waste heat

Copper heat spreader

Aluminum thermal plate

Infrared energy captured by the spectral layer



These components all transfer heat into the same circulating water, increasing overall thermal recovery.



8. Pumps and Controls



A variable-speed circulation pump moves water between the roof collectors and the storage tank.



Sensors monitor:



Roof collector temperature

Tank temperature

Indoor room temperature

Outdoor temperature



A central controller starts or stops circulation based on these measurements.



9. Home Temperature Control



The homeowner sets the desired indoor temperature using a programmable thermostat.



For example:



65°F (energy-saving)

68°F (standard)

72°F (comfort)

75°F (high comfort)



When the indoor temperature falls below the set point:



The thermostat signals the controller.

A circulation pump starts.

Hot water flows from the storage tank to the home's heating system.

Heat is delivered until the desired temperature is reached.

The pump slows or stops, conserving stored heat.



Multiple heating zones can be controlled independently, allowing different rooms or floors to have different temperatures.



Overall Energy Flow



Sunlight → Quartz lens → Spectral management layer → Photovoltaic cells generate electricity → Copper heat spreader collects waste heat → 
Thermal plate transfers heat → Copper tubing warms circulating fluid → Heated fluid flows through insulated pipes 
→ 1,000-gallon insulated thermal tank stores energy → Heat exchanger supplies radiant floors, radiators, or an air handler 
→ Thermostat-controlled circulation maintains the desired indoor temperature while electricity continues to power the home.



This architecture keeps the photovoltaic panels cooler for better electrical performance while simultaneously recovering useful
heat that would otherwise be lost, integrating electrical generation and thermal storage into a single rooftop energy system.



Stopped thinking

Edit



The biggest advantage of QPHES concept is not that it necessarily produces more energy than every competing technology.
Its strength is that it attempts to use much more of the sunlight that reaches the roof. Conventional solar panels convert
part of the sunlight into electricity, while much of the remaining energy becomes heat that actually reduces electrical efficiency. 
Your concept is designed to recover some of that heat and use it for the home instead of letting it escape.



Why recover heat?



A standard photovoltaic (PV) panel gets hot in the sun—often reaching 140–170°F (60–75°C) on warm days. Higher temperatures reduce
the electrical efficiency of silicon solar cells.



The QPHES concept addresses this by:



Producing electricity from the photovoltaic cells.

Removing excess heat with a circulating liquid loop.

Storing that heat in an insulated water tank.

Reusing the stored heat for space heating and domestic hot water.



This can provide two potential benefits:



Cooler PV cells, which can improve electrical output.

Useful thermal energy that would otherwise be wasted.



The actual improvement depends on the engineering design and would need to be validated through testing.



Compared with other heating methods

Heating Method	Advantages	Disadvantages

QPHES (Concept)	Uses sunlight for electricity and heat, quiet operation, low emissions during use, thermal storage can shift heat to nighttime	Higher installation cost, depends on collector area, storage size, and available sunlight

Firewood	Can provide very high heat output and work without electricity in some systems	Requires cutting or purchasing wood, smoke and particulate emissions, ash removal, chimney maintenance

Coal	High energy density	High carbon emissions, ash disposal, air pollution, generally being phased out for residential heating in many places

Why thermal storage can be valuable



One of the strongest ideas in your design is the thermal battery.



Instead of storing all energy as electricity in batteries:



Sun heats water during the day.

The insulated tank stores thermal energy.

The home uses the stored heat after sunset or overnight.



For space heating, storing heat in water is often much less expensive than storing the same amount of energy in electrochemical batteries.



Why water is a good heat storage medium



Water has a high specific heat capacity, meaning it can store a large amount of thermal energy for each degree of temperature increase.



A 1,000-gallon insulated tank can store a substantial amount of heat that can later be transferred into the home's heating system.



Where QPHES could have an advantage



A well-engineered QPHES system could be attractive because it combines multiple functions into one rooftop installation:



One roof structure

One plumbing loop

One electrical system

One thermal storage tank

One controller



Instead of installing separate systems for:



Solar electricity

Space heating support



the concept integrates these functions.



Compared with burning wood



Wood burns by converting chemical energy directly into heat.



A QPHES system collects renewable solar energy and stores it as heat without combustion.



Potential advantages of QPHES include:



No smoke during operation.

No ash.

No chimney.

No fuel deliveries.

No cutting or storing firewood.

Automatic operation.



Potential advantages of wood include:



Reliable heat during long periods of cloudy weather if fuel is available.

Very high peak heat output.

Can operate independently of sunlight.

Overall assessment



For a 2,000 sq ft home, the concept's biggest innovation is not simply "making more electricity." 
It is recovering heat that conventional PV panels largely reject and storing that heat for later use.
If successfully engineered, it could reduce the amount of purchased heating fuel or electricity needed
during sunny periods while also producing electricity.



Whether it would outperform today's best residential heating options depends on climate, system cost,
collector size, insulation, and control strategy. In many cold regions, a QPHES-style system would most
likely be evaluated against solar thermal systems, photovoltaic systems paired with heat pumps, or hybrid
renewable heating systems, since those are its closest real-world competitors. Extensive engineering analysis
and field testing would be needed to determine its performance and cost-effectiveness relative to those existing technologies.



Cooler in summer time?



Yes. In fact, summer cooling could become one of the strongest features of the QPHES concept.
There are several technically plausible ways to use the same rooftop thermal loop to reduce cooling loads.



Option 1: Active Panel Cooling (Recommended)



This is the simplest and most practical approach.



During summer:



The quartz lens allows sunlight to reach the photovoltaic cells.

The cells heat up.

The thermal pipes immediately remove that heat.

Instead of sending the heat into the house, it is diverted to:

the domestic hot water tank,

a swimming pool,

a seasonal thermal storage tank,

or another useful heat sink.



As a result:



PV cells operate at a lower temperature.

Electrical output can improve because silicon cells are more efficient when cooler.

Less heat radiates into the attic and roof structure.

Option 2: Radiant Cooling



Instead of circulating hot water through floor tubing, the system circulates cool water.



The cool water can flow through:



radiant ceiling panels,

radiant floors,

or specialized wall panels.



The room surfaces absorb heat from the occupants and furnishings, making the space feel cooler.



This approach requires careful control to avoid condensation.



Option 3: Geothermal Cooling



The rooftop loop could connect to an underground loop.



For example:



6–8 ft below ground, the soil remains around 50–60°F in many climates.

Water from the roof transfers excess heat into the earth.

Cooler water returns to the house.



This acts as a natural heat sink during summer.



Option 4: Seasonal Thermal Storage



Rather than rejecting heat, QPHES could store it.



Summer:



Roof → Thermal tank



Winter:



Thermal tank → House heating



Large district heating systems already use seasonal thermal storage, although storing enough heat
for an individual home through an entire season requires a much larger, well-insulated storage system than a typical residential tank.



Option 5: Absorption Cooling (Advanced)



This is the most advanced option.



Instead of using electricity to drive an air conditioner:



solar heat powers an absorption chiller,

the chiller produces chilled water,

chilled water cools the house.



This technology is used in some commercial buildings but is generally more complex and expensive than conventional residential air conditioning.



Intelligent QPHES Summer Mode



The controller could automatically switch between operating modes.



Winter

Capture heat.

Store heat.

Heat domestic water.

Heat the home.

Produce electricity.

Summer

Cool photovoltaic cells.

Produce electricity.

Heat domestic water.

Heat a swimming pool if available.

Reject excess heat to a geothermal loop or cooling tower if no heat demand exists.

Intelligent Thermal Routing



An automated valve system could prioritize where heat goes:



Domestic hot water tank.

Swimming pool.

Seasonal thermal storage.

Geothermal heat sink.

Outdoor heat rejection.



This way, useful heat is captured whenever possible instead of being wasted.



Additional Panel Improvements



You could also incorporate:



Microchannel copper cooling plates, which provide more uniform cooling than simple round tubing.

Graphite heat spreaders to distribute heat evenly across the back of the photovoltaic cells.

Phase-change materials (PCMs) integrated into the panel to temporarily absorb excess heat during rapid temperature spikes.

Variable-speed circulation pumps that increase flow when panel temperatures rise and slow down when less cooling is needed.

Overall Concept

 QPHES system would become an all-season energy management system rather than just a solar panel.



Winter



Generates electricity.

Captures solar heat.

Stores thermal energy.

Heats the house.

Provides domestic hot water.



Summer



Generates electricity.

Keeps the photovoltaic cells cooler for improved electrical performance.

Reduces heat entering the roof and attic.

Produces domestic hot water.

Can heat a pool or reject excess heat to the ground.

Can integrate with radiant cooling or other cooling technologies if desired.



That combination of electricity production, heat recovery, thermal storage, and intelligent heat routing is what 
distinguishes the QPHES concept from a conventional rooftop photovoltaic installation.







If the QPHES system is used only to remove heat from the roof and circulate that heat elsewhere, it will make
the attic and roof cooler, but it will not replace a conventional air conditioner by itself. To actively cool 
the living space to comfortable temperatures, it would need to be paired with a cooling technology such as 
radiant cooling, a heat pump, or an absorption chiller.

1,000-gallon thermal tank, and an active cooling system that uses the recovered heat effectively, a

Summer operation

Sunlight strikes the quartz lens.

The photovoltaic cells generate electricity.

Heat is conducted into the copper thermal plate.

Cool liquid flowing through the embedded copper tubing removes that heat.

The heated liquid is routed away from the roof so the panels remain cooler.

A controller decides where the heat should go:

Domestic hot water

Swimming pool

Seasonal thermal storage

Ground heat exchanger (geothermal loop)

Heat rejection radiator or cooling tower

If the house requires cooling, the home's cooling system removes heat from the indoor air while the QPHES
reduces roof heat gain and provides electricity to help power the equipment.

Why this helps



A dark roof exposed to full summer sun can reach 150–170°F. By actively removing heat from the panels, 
the roof surface and attic can be significantly cooler than they would otherwise be, reducing the heat
entering the home. That lowers the cooling load on the house and can improve photovoltaic electrical
output because the cells operate at a lower temperature.



A stronger version of the concept



An advanced QPHES system could combine:



Active rooftop liquid cooling

High-efficiency heat pump

Radiant ceiling cooling

Underground geothermal loop

1,000-gallon thermal storage tank

Smart thermostat with variable-speed pumps and valves



In that configuration, the QPHES panels primarily act as an energy management system—producing electricity, 
recovering heat, reducing rooftop temperatures, and supplying energy to the home's heating and cooling 
equipment—rather than directly air-conditioning the house on their own. This integrated approach is 
technically more realistic than expecting the rooftop thermal loop alone to provide all of the cooling.
